In this appendix we contextualize the main analysis in the MPX NYC project in a framework we name Social and Spatial Network Analysis with Causal interpretation, or SNACC. This framework allows for the analysis and causal interpretation of network data. It offers guidance on concpetualizing and organizing spatial and social data as network data, as well as describing that data using statistics.

The goals of this appendix are to motivate the usefulness of the SSNAC framework by describing the dominant system for investigating causality in the field of epidemiology, which we will call “classic causal inference”. We extend this system to situations where data are characterized by interdependancy rather than independence of observational units. First we accommodate social network data, naming this system “network causal inference”, and finally we accommodate social and spatial data simultaneously in a framework we named SSNAC. In each case, we describe the assumptions and implications of each system, illustrating with worked examples.

Classic causal inference, as articulated by ((Robins 1986; Hernán and Robins 2018)) and others helps us to sharpen epidemiologic reasoning by providing a template for conducting thought experiments about population health. Typically in these experiments, one considers what the effect of a hypothetical intervention would be on some outcome measured in a hypothetical population. The system allows the researcher to map this hypothetical experiment to data collected from (typically) people through explicit assumptions. Crucially, it allows the researcher to quantify the impact of the intervention on the outcome.

For instance, consider a hyothetical intervention where every person in the United States receives free, unlimited access to healthcare. Under classic causal inference, we can ask whether the prevalence of diabetes would change if we implemented the intervention. More specifically, we can ask about the extent of change and estimate it using data that have already been observed. The frameowrk allows this firstly by making causal assumptions about the relationship between healthcare and diabetes prevalence and secondly by stating the hypothetical quantities that are generated in the hypothetical experiment as a function of observed and measured quantities where possible. It allows us to estimate quantities that would arise under different conditions of the thought experiments.

A crucial assumption made almost universally by practitoiners of classic causal inference is that of independence between observational units or groups of observational units. In our opening example, this assumptoin would imply that one person’s access to healthcare does not affect any other person’s diabetes risk. To be pointed, tt would imply that the diabetes risk for a married man who does not know how to cook would not be affected by improvements in health literacy experienced by his wife who does. The assumption of independence runs counter to our intuitions about social life. Humans are networked creatures - our lives are defined by our relationships with others.

But the choice of adopting the independence assumption or not has more profound implications. In the frequentist tradition of statistics, the engine of uncertainty is repetition. We imagine that an experiment could be repeated over and over again and we summarize its behavior accross those repetitions. We do not often consider what exactly we mean by experiment and by repetition, though we make this distinction each time we decide how to analyse repeated measures data.

An example clarifies. Consider a situation with two people, Tumi and Thabo, who each toss the same coin once at time 1 and again at time 2. There are several ways of parsing this situation into “experiment” and “repetition”. We could say that: - we are conducting one experiment called “coin toss” and repeating it four times - we are conducting two experiments called “Thabo tosses a coin” and “Tumi tosses a coin” and are repeating each twice - we are conducting four experiments called “Thabo tosses a coin at time 1”, “Thabo tosses a coin at time 2”, “Tumi tosses a coin at time 1”, and “Tumi tosses a coin at time 2, and got to observe each of these once.

The choice between these interpretations turns on the experimenters prior beliefs about coin tossing: Does the likelihood of a head or tail change over time? Does it change based on the person? And does the result of one person’s coin toss affect the result of another? This kind of decision-making is at the core statistical modelling. Yet, in practice, the modelling decision to categorize people as independent of each other is so well-entrenched that it is often represented as a minimum condition of doing any valid causal quantitative research. Our goal in this appendix and project is to offer alternative ideas and tools for causal inference that allow us to learn form interdependence rather than fear it.

In crude terms, our approach involves moving from a framework where we consider the coin toss example one experiment, rather than break it up into two or four. We represent the whole experiement, therfore, as a vector of length 4 that comes from some joint distribution. But why would a researcher want to do that when asymptotic theory requires us to have as many samples as possible? How can we conduct valid statistical inference and measure uncertainty when we always have a sample size of 1?

Briefly, we do that because a model which considers the coin toss example to be only one experiment is correct even if it turns out that the four coin tosses are independent of each other. We make the assumption of no-spillover between observations, which is that one person’s outcome can not direcetly affect another person’s outcome. This assumption is weaker than the independence assumption. We further assume that the causal relatiohships between and within individuals are homogenous in some ways, allowing our estimates to benefit from larger sample sizes. As it will turn out, under certain homogeneity assumptions, asymptotic theory can be used to improve estimates in a growing vector of inter-related observations, whereas it is usally applied to a growing collection of vectors of equal length

In the sections that follow, we describe classic causal inference, network causal infernce, and the SSNAC framework, each time discussing the assumptions, observed and counterfactual data, causal estimands, add identification and estimation. Finally, we describe the MPX NYC outbreak response analysis using the SSNAC framework and end with a brief summary of the appendix.

Example 1: Wealth, Dress, and Jobs

We are interested in settling an argument among friends about the relationship between dressing in formal wear and job opportunities. One friend thinks that dressing formally clearly brings more job opportunities; everyone she knows who dresses formally has many more opportunities than people who dress casually. The other friend thinks that what really brings job opportunities is currently having a job. In their reasoning, having a job causes people to dress formally and causes people to have job opportunities, but dressing formally does not in itself improve opportunities. We conduct a study to try investigate.

We recruit three people, Person 1, Person 2, and Person 3. Person 1 and Person 2 are married to each other. Person 1 and Person 3 are newly acquainted colleagues. Say Person 1 has job status \(L_1\), Person 2 has job status \(L_2\) and Person 3 has job status \(L_3\). Each of these variables can only take on two values: “employed vs. unemployed”. Similarly we define \(A_i\) as Person i’s dress (formal vs. informal), and \(Y_i\) as their job opportunities (high vs. low). The job status measurement precedes \(L_i\) dress \(A_i\) in time, and dress precedes job opportunities \(Y_i\).

If we made no assumptions at all about the causal process, except the time-ordering of measurements, we can represent our data using ?eq-nospillover-dag. Note that each arrow in the DAG represents an argument in the function that produces the variable the arrow is pointing to.

A directed acyclic graph (DAG) is a representation of a non-parametric structural equation model. It is a useful representation because it allows us to graphically find the minimum set of conditional independencies that imply all the conditional Independence in the distribution. Conditional independencies are identified by missing arrows in the DAG, using a set of rules called D-separation.

Since there are no missing edges between nodes in the Figure D.1, the structural equation model represents any possible joint distribution between the variables in the DAG - there are no conditional independencies. But the most flexible model is not the most useful one. To fully characterize this system using observed data, we would need to estimate 9 functions using one observation of the 9-dimensional joint distribution that gave rise to the participant’s observations. It would not help to recruit additional people to the study because each person would add three new equations to the model.

D.1.1 Assumptions

Under classic causal inference, we assume that observations are independent and identically distributed. We review these assumptions, naming them slightly differently for the purpose of comparing them with the assumptions made in sections to come. We assume independence, we assume “no spillover” and “no interference”. We further classify the “identically distributed” assumption as a homogeneity assumption.

Independence assumptions

No spillover

Under classic causal inference, we make the assumption of no spillover - that measurements taken across participants at the same time (e.g. \(L_1\), \(L_2\), and \(L_3\)) are independent of each other given all prior measures. i.e.

\[ \begin{align} L_1 \perp L_2 \\ L_2 \perp L_3 \\ L_1 \perp L_3 \end{align} \] This is shown in Figure D.2, as the removal of arrows that connected the \(L_i\)’s, \(A_i\)’s and \(Y_i\)’s. In \(L_2\) is no longer a function of \(L_1\), for instance. In Equation D.2, the \(L_i\)’s are not functions of each other, the \(A_i\)’s are not functions of each other, and the \(Y_i\)’s are not functions of each other. This implies that given all the measurements taken prior to a set of contemporaneous measures, the contemporaneous measures are independent.

No interference

Classic causal inference makes the no interference assumption. We assume that measurements made across participants, but not at the same time are independent of each other. This is shown as the absence of arrows that connect \(L_i\)’s to \(A_j\)’s, \(L_i\)’s to \(Y_j\)’s and \(A_i\)’s to \(Y_j\) for participants \(i,j\) with \(i \neq j\)

This allows us to simplify the structural equation model so that each participants measures are a function of only measures taken on them.

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}( U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}( U_{L_2}) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}( U_{L_3}) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}( U_{A_1}, L_1) \\ A_2 &= f_{A_2}( U_{A_2}, L_2) \\ A_3 &= f_{A_3}( U_{A_3}, L_3) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y_1}( U_{Y_1}, L_1, A_1 ) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y_2}( U_{Y_2}, L_2, A_2 ) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y_3}( U_{Y_3}, L_3, A_3 ) \\ \end{aligned} \]

Homogeneity assumption

Identical distribution

Finally, we see above that the functions that generate observations \(L_i\) are similar in structure to each other, as are the the functions that generate \(A_i\)’s and \(Y_i\)s. We make the homogeneity assumption that the functions are identical to each other. i.e.

\[\begin{aligned} f_L &= f_{L_1} = f_{L_2} = f_{L_3} \\ f_A &= f_{A_1} = f_{A_2} = f_{A_3} \\ f_Y &= f_{Y_1} = f_{Y_2} = f_{Y_3} \\ \end{aligned}\]

This allows us to re-write the structural equation model dropping the person subscript from the equations. The model simplifies into one for three measures each taken on one person, rather than a model for 9 measures taken on 3 people. Since the measures on each person are identically distributed, this model can be used three times to produce the measures for person 1, person 2, and person 3. The original model produced measures for all three people at one go.

D.1.5 Identification and estimation

Once we have specified the hypothetical quantities from our thought experiment, what is left is to estimate them directly from the observed data. It is enough to identify \(E[Y_i^{(a)}|\mathbf{L}]\) to calculate the hypothetical quantity given by the estimand with observed data.

\[\begin{aligned} E[Y_i^{(a)}|\mathbf{L}] &= E[ Y_i^{(a)} | \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{L}, \mathbf{A} = a \times \mathbf{1}] \\ &= E[ Y_i^{(a)} | L_i = l_i , A_i = a ] \\ &= E[ f_{Y}(U_{Y_i} , l_i, a) | L_i = l_i , A_i = a ] \\ &= E[ Y_i | L_i = l_i, A_i = a, ] \\ \end{aligned}\]

In the first line, we use the fact that since it is constant, the value of dress set by the experimenter \(a\) is independent of the observed value of dress \(A_i\) for each person \(i\). In the second, we use the no spillover and no interference assumptions - person \(i\)’s values (including counterfactual values) are independent of any measures not taken on person \(i\). In the third line we susbtitute \(Y_i^{(a)}\) for the structural equation that produces that variable, according to the model.

Identical distribution

As in the case of classical causal inference, we assume that the structual equations that produce each individual’s observations are identical accross individuals. i.e. we assume:

\[\begin{aligned} f_A = f_{A_1} &= f_{A_2} = f_{A_3} \\ f_L = f_{L_1} &= f_{L_2} = f_{L_3} \\ f_Y = f_{Y_1} &= f_{Y_2} = f_{Y_3} \\ \end{aligned}\]
Homogenous interference

Separability

We assume that in each structural equation, each covariate contributes to the exposure mapping in two parts: i) the covariate level for the individual and ii) a covariate level of the ego-network of that individual.

Define \(\mathscr{N_i}\) as the indices of all the nodes connected to node \(i\). Then \(\mathbf{L}_{\mathscr{N}_i}\) is the sub-vector of \(\mathbf{L}\) containing the entries corresponding to the indices in \(\mathscr{N}_i\). Similarly, we define \(A_{\mathscr{N}_i}\) as the sub-vector of \(A\) corresponding to the indices in \(\mathscr{N}_i\).

\[ \begin{aligned} L_1 &= f_{L_1}(U_{L_1}) \\ L_2 &= f_{L_2}(U_{L_2}) \\ L_3 &= f_{L_3}(U_{L_3} ) \\ A_1 &= f_{A_1}(U_{A_1}, L_1, g_{A_1}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathscr{N}_1})) \\ A_2 &= f_{A_2}(U_{A_2}, L_2, g_{A_2}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathscr{N}_2})) \\ A_3 &= f_{A_3}(U_{A_3}, L_3, g_{A_3}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathscr{N}_3})) \\ Y_1 &= f_{Y_1}(U_{Y_1}, L_1, A_1, g_{Y_1}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathscr{N}_1}), h_{Y_1}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathscr{N}_1})) \\ Y_2 &= f_{Y_2}(U_{Y_2}, L_2, A_2, g_{Y_2}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathscr{N}_2}), h_{Y_2}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathscr{N}_2})) \\ Y_3 &= f_{Y_3}(U_{Y_3}, L_3,A_3, g_{Y_2}(\mathbf{L}_{\mathscr{N}_3}), h_{Y_2}(\mathbf{A}_{\mathscr{N}_3})) \\ \end{aligned} \]

This simplifies the model, but still forces us to write the structural equations for each observation separately since each observation potentially has a set of unique exposure mappings.

Equivalency

We assume that exposure mappings \(g_A\), \(h_y\), and \(g_Y\) are identical across individuals.

\[\begin{aligned} g_A = g_{L_1} &= g_{L_2} = g_{L_3} \\ h_Y = h_{A_1} &= h_{A_2} = h_{A_3} \\ g_Y = g_{L_1} &= g_{L_2} = g_{L_3} \\ \end{aligned}\]

Symmetry

We also assume that the order of the entries does not matter in the the input for functions \(g_A\), \(g_Y\), and \(h_Y\). i.e. the functions are invariant under any permutation of the entries in the input vector.

Boundedness

We assume that exposure mappings are bounded above.

\[ g_A, h_Y, g_Y < c \text{ for } c \in \mathbf{Z} \]

Example 3: Longitudinal Data-Driven Vaccination Campaign

Imagine a vaccination campaign conducted over three weeks in the city of Nowhereville. Five people live in the city of Nowhereville. They each live in a particular neighborhood, but visit other neighborhoods as well. You are interested in promoting flu vaccination, but want to make sure you understand the likely pattern of flu transmission based on where people live and visit.

Person 1 lives in Neighborhood A and visit Neighborhood B. Person 2 lives in Neighborhood B, but vists Neighborhoods A and B. Person 3 lives in Neighborhood C and visits Neighborhood B. Persons 5 and 6 live in Neighborhood C and they do not visit any other neighborhoods.

In this system, at the beginnig of each week, each neighborhood gets a priority ranking based on the prevalence of vaccination, among other factors. Based on that ranking, each neighborhood will either implement the invitation (if its ranking is high enough), or not. At the person level, each person has an indicator of whether they have been exposed to the messaging or not. If they have, and they have not yet vaccinated, they get another follow-up message. If they have been vaccinated, and have receive a message, they have vaccinated. no further messages are delivered to them. Finally, we have a measure indicating whether each individual was vaccinated or not. Once an individual is vaccinated, they remain vaccinated.

D.3.3 Counterfactual data

Assuming that the public health system usually works in the way described in the observed data section, we can imagine different combinations of intervention and outcome. We could ask what would happen if we intervened to set exposure to the campaign message at the individual level (for example, by randomly selecting participants to directly contact), or we could ask what would happen if we intervened to conduct the intervention in some communities but not others. These would both be node-level interventions. For each of these questions, we could focus either on person-level outcomes like vaccination status or community-level outcomes like vaccination prevalence. Finally, we could ask what would happen if we intervened to change the relationship between to nodes in the DANG. By asking, for instance, what would happen if the city department of health changed how it ranked neighborhoods for priority. We discuss each of these three intervention scenarios in more detail below.

Place intervention

Alternatively, say we were interested in a community-level intervention such as the vaccination messaging campaign we diescribed above. In particular, say we want to understand the impact of a vaccination campaign conducted over three time periods. This would be done by setting \(\mathbf{T}_1\) to \(\mathbf{t}_1\), \(\mathbf{T}_2\) to \(\mathbf{t}_2\), and \(\mathbf{T}_3\) to \(\mathbf{t}_3\). D.4.2 Causal estimands

Spatial efficiency

Intervention coverage for the \(s^{th}\) community district to be immunized, is defined as:

\[ C_s = \frac{1}{|N_p|}\sum_i V_{si}^{(h)} \]

The cumulative intervention coverage after immunizing \(s\) community districts, therefore, is:

\[ \bar{C_s} = \sum_{v = 1}^s{C_v } \]

Social network impact

Define graph \(G^{s}(N, \phi^s)\) such that

\[ \phi_s(i, j) = \phi(i, j) \times (1 -V_{(s-1), i}) \]

The cumulative social network impact of immunizing \(s\) community districts is measured in terms of the size of the largest of the connected components \(LCC^{(s)}\) of the \(G^{s}(N, \phi^s)\) network as a proportion of the total number of participants.

\[ \frac{LCC_s}{|N_p|} \]

D.5 Statistical inference

To conduct the bootstrap, we start by forming a synthetic dataset \(N_p^*\) of re-sampled individuals. To do this, we repeatedly sample a random individual \(t\) from the set of people \(N_p\) with replacement. Then we assign each sampled individual a new index using function \[ l: N \to N^* \]

Using the sampled individuals and all the community districts, we form a new network

\[ G(N^*, \psi^*) \]

where \(N^* = N_p^* \cup N_c\). In this graph, the adjacency function satisfies:

\[ \psi^*(a,b) = \psi(l^{-1}(a), b) \] for \(a \in N_p^*\) and \(b \in N_c\).

In \(G(N^*, \psi^*)\), individuals are batched into equal groups. Each group represent a repetition of the bootstrap procedure. Then for a given statistic, statistical inference is implemented by calculating the statistic separately in each of these groups and treating the result as a random realization from the distribution of that particular statistic.
