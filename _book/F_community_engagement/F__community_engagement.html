E - MPX NYC I: Organizing

E.1 RESPND-MI Community forum

Starting in May 2022, we prepared a study about sexual activity and mpox symptoms among those most vulnerable to mpox. To promote consultation and collective decision making for the study, we organised the RESPND-MI LGBTQ+ Community Forum (CF), a weekly convening via Zoom. In the first CF, attendees expressed the need for a space to continually coordinate response efforts across organisations and to address gaps not immediately met by local and federal public health agencies.

CFs thus became weekly calls where participants received epidemiologic updates, discussed problems, and provided input on study design and marketing decisions. From June to September 2022, over 80 activists, community leaders, and public health professionals from across the country participated in CFs. We produced policy briefs, op-eds, and educational materials and conducted media outreach. The RESPND-MI website became a clearinghouse for community-generated resources, as well as for community-generated information on vaccination, prevention, testing, and treatment services. Educational materials and policy recommendations from the CF were adopted by city, state, and federal public health agencies. Reporters cited RESPND-MI in over 90 press clips. Survey data were used to inform the New York City Department of Health’s mobile vaccination outreach services (Roth et al. 2023).

Roth, Grant, Jennifer Barnes-Balenciaga, Joseph Osmundson, Martez DR Smith, Nguyen K Tran, Nicholas Diamond, and Keletso Makofane. 2023. “Global North Learning from Global South: A Community-Led Response to Mpox in New York City.” PLOS Global Public Health 3 (6): e0002042.

The CF shaped study plans, including the recruitment campaign, the design of a novel spatial data collection tool, and the development of the study questionnaire. Sustained engagement led to several concrete improvements in the research approach. For example, the CF requested for the study questionnaire and communication materials to be translated from English to Spanish, and the CF selected the Fire Island ferry terminal and LinkNYC kiosks for displaying out-of-home advertisements about RESPND-MI. Additionally, a criticism of the initial communication approach was that it centered cisgender men to the exclusion of transgender people. This was consistent with most communication about the social epidemiology of mpox at the time and most scientific reporting.

But given that many public health data systems use measures for gender that are unable to identify transgender individuals, limiting attention to cisgender men would have limited our ability to understand the true extent of the mpox outbreak. We assumed that transgender people and cisgender people are part of the same social and sexual networks which would likely be affected by mpox. We invited the participant who raised that criticism to join the study team. Over time, we attempted to continually improve gender-related language and iconography in the project through discussion among the CF and co-investigators.

E.2 Presentations and workshops

The RESPND-MI study team actively shared insights and findings through a diverse range of abstracts, presentations, panels, and workshops, reaching audiences across local, national, and international platforms. Martez Smith, Keletso Makofane, and Jennifer Barnes-Balenciaga spoke directly about RESPND-MI’s work in New York City at social events hosted by The Keeping Ballroom Community Alive Network (KBCAN), fostering critical dialogue within ballroom and house communities most affected by mpox. Additionally, the RESPND-MI team played a key role in organizing and leading protests demanding equitable mpox vaccine access during the 24th International AIDS Conference, highlighting the urgent need for global solidarity in public-health responses.

Beyond these community-based engagements, the team presented at numerous professional conferences and academic institutions. RESPND-MI contributed to the National LGBTQ+ Bar Association’s program with “Love, Law, Art, and Activism in the Time of Mpox” and advanced methodological training for epidemiologists through the Society for Epidemiological Research session “Single World Intervention Graphs (SWIGs) for the Practicing Epidemiologist.” The team also shared findings and strategies in the session “We Protect Us: Understanding Mpox as a Public Health Emergency and Co-creating Solutions in a Key Populations-Led Response” with Frontline AIDS.

The presentation “1,112 and Counting: Lessons From HIV/AIDS While Responding to Mpox in Queer and Trans Communities” was delivered across multiple venues, including Boston University School of Public Health, the Elizabeth Glaser Pediatric AIDS Foundation, and Albion College, each time drawing parallels between the HIV/AIDS epidemic and the contemporary mpox response to emphasize enduring lessons in health equity and community mobilization. At Boston University School of Public Health, RESPND-MI also participated as a panelist in the session “Monkeypox, Old Disease, New Fears,” exploring historical and social dynamics fueling stigma and misinformation.

Internationally, the team presented “Mobilising a Rapid and Comprehensive Community-led Response to MPOX: Lessons From the RESPND-MI Study Team” and “Community-based Responses to Health Emergencies: The Case of Mpox and the RESPND-MI Study in New York City” at the 12th International AIDS Society Conference on HIV Science, positioning the study as a model for rapid, community-driven responses to emerging public-health crises. Additional forums included AVAC’s panel “Mpox – Sexual Networks, HIV and Activism” and a session titled “MPX NYC — Lessons Learnt and Next Steps” for the World Health Organization, where RESPND-MI’s work informed ongoing conversations about global readiness and the integration of sexual-health expertise into outbreak response. Through this extensive dissemination, the RESPND-MI team contributed both practical tools and critical reflections to diverse audiences, advancing the field’s understanding of how to effectively engage queer and trans communities during public-health emergencies.
