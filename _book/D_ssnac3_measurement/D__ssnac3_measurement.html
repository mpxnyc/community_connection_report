We describe the process of designing and testing a novel survey questionnaire tool we call the MPX NYC person-place mapper. We show the questions participants were presented with in the survey.

D.1 Person–Place Network Mapper

We designed, tested, and implemented the MPX NYC Person–Place Network Mapper to address practical and ethical challenges in collecting sensitive spatial data in an anonymous survey. Our aim was to capture information on venues participants visited, including those where group sex occurred, while minimizing the risk of re-identification through location data.

D.1.1 Place Mapping

Because participants were unlikely to recall precise addresses or zip codes, we used the Google Maps API to let them identify locations by landmarks, cross-streets, subway stations, and neighborhood names. The map displayed detailed geographic information with standard navigation tools (see Figure D.1).

Participants were prompted to identify specific types of places, such as their primary residence, and then to select additional locations where they had physical or sexual contact in group settings. Each tap on the map placed a marker, which triggered follow-up questions about activity at that site and the type of venue.

D.1.2 Link Tracing

We developed a referral system to build a network anonymously. At the start of the survey, participants received a unique identifier card containing a QR code and word combination, which they were asked to save.

At the end of the survey, participants could share a pre-written invitation with a unique URL. If a contact used the link, an edge was added between the two respondents in the graph database. When a new participant connected with someone who had already completed the survey, the existing participant could confirm their identifier card, allowing the system to link their nodes without revealing identities.

D.1.3 Implementation

To reduce re-identification risk, spatial data were coarsened before storage. For example, coordinates were converted to census tract identifiers, preventing disclosure of exact locations such as a dormitory or government office.

The tool’s front end was built as a single-page React/NextJS application hosted on Vercel, with design based on the MPX NYC style guide. The back end used a custom Neo4j graph database. Standard survey formats (multiple choice, text, etc.) were integrated alongside the mapping and referral components.

Data flow proceeded as follows: the front end loaded questions from a JSON file, parsed referral identifiers and language settings from the URL, and requested a public identifier from the API. The API generated and stored public/private identifier pairs in a secure table, returned the public identifier and QR code to the participant, and transformed identifiers before sending responses to the graph database.

For spatial inputs, the front end queried the FCC API, which returned census block identifiers. These were truncated to census tract identifiers before being stored in the study database. Two rounds of user testing with about ten participants each informed refinements to usability and clarity.
