Principles

The success of the RESPND-MI study was grounded in a set of deeply held principles that guided every aspect of its formation and operation. At its core was a commitment to community empowerment—the belief that queer and trans communities possess the expertise and insight needed to understand and address their own health challenges. Rather than treating community members as informants or subjects, the study positioned them as co-investigators and decision-makers, capable of shaping research design, communication strategies, and interpretation of findings. This approach drew inspiration from Global South frameworks of health activism that emphasize sex positivity, mutual trust, and community ownership of knowledge. Transparency and accountability were essential: data were collected and used first and foremost to serve the community, rather than to fulfill institutional mandates.

The group also operated on a set of internal principles that sustained its collaborative energy during the emergency. Participation was designed to bring joy rather than stress, ensuring that contributors felt energized by their involvement. The organizing culture embraced experimentation and low-stakes failure, recognizing that in moments of crisis, iteration and imperfection are essential to learning. The group’s ethos of “abundance and redundancy” valued overlapping initiatives as sources of resilience rather than inefficiency. A commitment to democracy ensured that decisions were made collectively and transparently, while the principle of “building as we fly” allowed for adaptability in rapidly changing conditions. Finally, the team maintained a constant awareness of power and history. By explicitly situating their work within a decolonial critique of global health, the investigators sought to reverse the traditional direction of knowledge transfer—from the Global South to the Global North—and to demonstrate that community-led monitoring and accountability could strengthen, rather than oppose, scientific rigor.

Practices

RESPND-MI translated these principles into practice through participatory co-design, distributed leadership, iterative, task-oriented collaboration, and a tight project management system that balanced autonomy with accountability. The LGBTQ+ Community Forum (CF), launched in May 2022, became the centerpiece of this participatory infrastructure. Initially conceived as a space for consultation, the CF rapidly evolved into a coordination mechanism that brought together activists, community leaders, public-health professionals, and researchers for weekly meetings. Over the course of the mpox emergency, more than eighty participants contributed to discussions and decisions that directly shaped the study. Forum members proposed the translation of the survey and outreach materials into Spanish, advised on the inclusion of transgender participants and iconography, selected outreach sites such as the Fire Island ferry terminal and LinkNYC kiosks, and helped refine messaging to avoid stigma. Through these interactions, the CF not only informed the research but also modeled a new form of shared governance that blurred the line between community organizing and epidemiologic investigation.

Internally, RESPND-MI’s tight project management system combined structure and flexibility. Teams worked from shared task lists that clarified responsibilities, timelines, and dependencies. Each participant tracked the status of their own work, marking tasks as complete or pending and reassigning them when necessary. This approach created clear lines of accountability without constraining creativity. Weekly investigator meetings served as synchronization points where team members reviewed feedback from the Community Forum, coordinated cross-functional work, and maintained momentum. These meetings also preserved space for informal connection and reflection—key ingredients for trust and sustained collaboration under crisis conditions.

RESPND-MI operated through distributed leadership rather than hierarchy. Authority was shared across functional leads who coordinated distinct domains of work while remaining accountable to the collective. The Principal Investigator anchored the overall vision and ethical integrity of the project, while the Research Coordinator maintained its rhythm and logistics. The Communications Lead shaped external messaging, the Questionnaire and Translation Leads ensured inclusivity and linguistic accessibility, and the Web Development Lead managed the group’s digital infrastructure. This networked form of leadership ensured that decisions were informed by diverse expertise, that no single bottleneck slowed the process, and that community voices remained central to every stage of design and implementation.

The organizing culture emphasized making things quickly and collectively. Rather than waiting for perfect consensus, teams prioritized the rapid production of useful outputs—policy briefs, educational materials, safer-sex guidance, vaccination locators, and op-eds—that directly met community needs in real time. The group’s motto—“talk to people and make things”—captured its pragmatic rhythm: ongoing dialogue with communities fed directly into the creation of tangible tools, which in turn generated new conversations and collaborations. Dissemination was integral to this practice, not a postscript. The team engaged with press outlets, government partners, and academic audiences to amplify community expertise as public-health expertise, presenting at venues ranging from ballroom community events to the World Health Organization and the International AIDS Society.

Structures

Beneath these principles and practices lay a set of organizational structures that made coordination possible. The investigator group organized its work around four core functions: coordination, surveillance, health communication, and resourcing. Coordination focused on gathering and sharing information among stakeholders, documenting the experiences of community-based organizations, and aligning programmatic activities across sectors. Surveillance addressed the group’s data needs—identifying gaps, synthesizing information, and making data accessible to both stakeholders and the public. Health communication combined the development of new materials with amplification of messages generated within communities themselves. Resourcing sustained the other three functions by identifying and mobilizing support from queer-serving companies, donors, foundations, and public agencies.

Together, these functions formed a dynamic ecosystem in which the Community Forum and the Investigator Group worked in tandem. The forum served as an open, participatory sphere for discussion and collective decision-making, while the investigator group translated that input into operational and scientific outputs. The arrangement allowed RESPND-MI to act both as a research project and as a coordination platform for community response. This structure enabled speed without sacrificing inclusivity or rigor: decisions were made collectively, outputs were produced iteratively, and community feedback remained at the center of every process.

The project’s dissemination network extended across scales. Locally, the team collaborated with the New York City Department of Health, community-based organizations, and cultural networks such as ballroom houses. Nationally, it contributed to advocacy initiatives like the Choice Agenda and AVAC’s panels on mpox and HIV activism. Internationally, the study was presented as a model of rapid, community-led response at venues including the WHO and the International AIDS Society. Through these efforts, RESPND-MI demonstrated that a decentralized, joyful, and accountable public-health research collective could operate as effectively—and often more equitably—than traditional institutions.

Conclusion

In sum, the RESPND-MI study succeeded because it wove together a coherent set of principles, practices, and structures that mutually reinforced one another. Its guiding principles—community empowerment, joy and democracy in participation, accountability, and decolonization—defined a moral and epistemic stance. Its participatory and distributed practices turned those ideals into action through co-design, shared leadership, iterative making, and disciplined project management. Its organizational structures provided the scaffolding that allowed those practices to scale and sustain themselves under the pressure of a public-health emergency. The result was a form of community-led epidemiologic inquiry that not only generated data but transformed the conditions under which data are produced, shared, and acted upon. RESPND-MI stands as evidence that community power, when structured intentionally and supported with care, can yield both scientific insight and collective liberation.
