MPX NYC

Key findings and recommendations

Author

Keletso Makofane

Methods

Data Collection

The Rapid Epidemiologic Study of Prevalence, Networks, and Demographics of Monkeypox Infection (RESPND-MI) implemented an anonymous online survey from 20 August 2022 to 15 November 2022 among transgender people and gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men in New York City.

RESPND-MI Community Forum

Investigators sought to consult with as many stakeholders who serve the health and human rights of queer and trans people in the New York City. We listed important organizations and collectives in the city and identified individuals within them. We divided this list of 53 key stakeholders among investigators and sent personalized invitations to a consultative meeting. For each name on the list, we tried to identify the investigator with the closest relationship and asked that investigator to send the invitation email.

On 27 June 2022 we held an initial consultative meeting, sharing the goals of the study. During the meeting, participants expressed a need to have a regular meeting that could be used to share updates on the unfolding mpox outbreak in New York City and how it is affecting health programs. The meeting came to be called the RESPND-MI Community Forum - a weekly public video conference that ran until 1 August 2022. We consulted with the RESPND-MI Community Forum on key study-related decisions throughout this period. About 80 people participated in the weekly call in total.

Recruitment

We collaborated with a marketing agency to develop and implement a study recruitment and health information campaign in English and Spanish. The brief was to create a name and visual identity for the campaign to convey the scientific rigor of the study, build trust with the community we aimed to survey, and to stand out in a crowded online advertising space (see Figure 1). As a starting point, we suggested that the agency develop a campaign that playfully communicates using emojis that have sexual connotations on online hookup sites. Four marketing concepts (see Figure 2) and two campaign names were developed by the marketing agency and presented to the RESPND-MI Community Forum. The final concept and name were decided in that meeting by consensus.

Marketing materials were developed based on the marketing concept. These included graphics for paid mobile ads, out-of-home ads, social media toolkit (which included videos and graphics for sharing through social media, along with suggested captions in English and Spanish), a style guide for all public-facing materials of the MPX NYC study (including colors, icons, and fonts), and videos by paid queer influencers. Influencers were chosen from a long-list created by one of the investigators during a meeting of the community forum.

Paid advertisements were placed through Twitter, and Instagram, and on an electronic billboard at the Fire Island Ferry Terminal in New York City. LinkNYC and Grindr advertisements were provided as donations-in-kind.

Data collection instrument

We designed and implemented a novel online survey instrument which captures detailed spatial information on important physcial locations while maintaining the participants’ anonymity. The instrument underwent two rounds of informal user testing before data collection.

Front end

The look and feel of the data collection instrument was based on the MPX NYC style guide. Along with standard question formats (multiple choice, single choice, and text field etc.) this instrument uses a novel question format (which we call the map-input question format here) to elicit spatial information.

In this question format, participants are prompted to indicate all places of a particular type by tapping on a google maps-like map (see Figure 3). The user can use drag and pinch gestures to shift or zoom in and out of the map, and can search for neighborhood names, place names, street addresses, to change the area shown on the map. When the user taps on the map, a marker is shown on the map, and a pop-up window asks them to confirm the location. If they do confirm, they answer a series of questions related to that location. Once all questions relating to a venue are answered, the user is allowed to tap on the map again to indicate a second venue, etc., or move on to the next question.

Back end

For each place that is indicated on the map, the google maps API (CITE) returns the latitude and longitude to the user’s device. The coordinates are sent to the Federal Communications Commission API (https://geo.fcc.gov/api/census/) which returns the identifier for the census block which contains the spatial coordinates. On the user’s device, the census black identifier is transformed into a census tract identifier by truncating the last X digits (CITE). Census tracts are a spatial unit that exhaustively divides the united states up into areas ranging from XX to XXX in population size (CITE). Data were stored in a custom-built graph database system hosted on a commercial cloud server platform.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was designed to take less than 5 minutes to take. A team of investigators developed the initial draft and circulated it among the investigator team for review and comment. The near-final questionnaire was reviewed by four researchers outside of the study team. It was implemented in English and Spanish.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Potential participants were included if they consented to participate, were over 18 years of age, identified as transgender, gay, bisexual, or who are men who have sex with other men, and were in New York City in the four weeks preceding the survey. Potential participants were excluded if they were younger than 18 years of age or they withdrew consent.

Translation

All study materials were professionally translated from English to Spanish. The translation was reviewed by six professionals in health-related fields whose primary language is Spanish.

Ethical Review

The study was reviewed and approved by the T.H. Chan School of Public Health Institutional Review Board (IRB22-0761).

Measures

Participants

We solicited information on participant sociodemographics, utilization of healthcare services such as pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), HIV treatment and vaccination against MPOX; connection to queer or transgender community through sex and socializing; and location of primary residence.

Gender identity was measured using a two-step approach: first, we solicited the participant’s current gender (man, woman, trans man, trans woman, non-binary, Other), and then their sex assigned at birth (male, female, other). Those whose gender identity was not concordant with the gender commonly associated with their sex assigned at birth were categorized as transgender. We used current gender to further categorize transgender people into men and women. Race was measured using a multiple selection question based on census categories (White, Black, Latino/a/x, Asian, Pacific Islander, Other). We defined gender-race subgroup as the combination of gender identity and race, collapsing small cells across racial group (White Cisgender Man, Latinx Cisgender Man, Black Cisgender Man, Other Cisgender Man, Non-Binary Person, Transgender Man, Transgender Woman, Cisgender Woman, Another Demographic).

Residence and Contact venues

We solicited the location of participants’ primary residence. We also asked whether participants had engaged in group sex or had physical contact in a congregate setting in the four weeks before taking the survey. Among those who answered positively, we solicited the locations of the venues at which the contact happened. We refer to these locations as contact venues.

For each contact venue, participants indicated the type of place it was (Dance party, Sex Party, Darkroom, Sport Game, Concert, Theatre/Show, Private Residence, Sex Club, Bathhouse, Park, Something Else) and whether they had sexual (vs. non-sexual physical) contact there.

Statistical Analysis

Participant characteristics

We tabulated participant responses to questions about healthcare access, connection with community, and demographics by report of contact. We also tabulated the characteristic of contact venues. We visualized the flow of study participants between home and contact venues using a matrix plot. We plotted this measure for subgroups defined by race-gender, sexual orientation, and age.

Contact venue characteristics

We converted the census tract identifiers on all contact venues into community district identifiers as publiched by New York City XXXX (CITATION). We visualized the distribution of contact venue types by distance from home and by sexual contact. Distance from home was approximated through a three-level ordinal variable with with levels (same community as home, different community but same borough as home, different borough than home).

Spatial mixing

To understand how the contact venues and residences of different kinds of participants are clustered in space, we constructed a spatial overlap network. In this network, nodes are participants and weighted ties indicate that the connected pair of individuals share at least one community district. Participants are said to share a community district if they each have a contact venue or residence in the same community district. The weight of the tie is proportional to the number of community districts they share.

The spatial overlap ratio from characteristic X to characteristic Y is the ratio of the conditional probability of someone with characteristics X sharing a community district with someone with characteristic Y with the marginal probability of having characteristic Y. The spatial coefficient is defined as this ratio less 1.

As a crude measure of the distance between the contact venue and participant residence, we categorized contact venues into those that are in the same community as the residence, the same borough but different community, or a different borough than the participant’s residence.

Intervention efficiency and network impact

We examined the efficiency and transmission impact of a hypothetical intervention to prioritize community district according to the amount of contact and vaccinate those districts in order of that priorization. We assume that vaccinating a community district means rendering every individuals who lives in or has had contact in that community district unable to acquire or transmit a hypothetical pathogen.

Efficiency was examined by understanding what the minimum number of community districts to be vaccinated to cover 33% of the population, and separately to cover 66% of the population.

Transmission impact was examined by understanding the impact of removing community districts which have been vaccinated on the spatial overlap network.

Statistical Inference

We used the non-parametric bootstrap to calculate point estimates and 95% confidence intervals (CI). Participants were re-sampled with replacement 100 times. For each measure, the lower and upper bound of confidence intervals were calculated as the 0.025th and 0.975th quantiles of the empirical distribution produced by re-sampling.
